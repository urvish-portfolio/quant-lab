US equities · 2000 to 2026

A short-term reversal in US stocks, tested on sealed data

Validation: narrow pass

Does a well-known reversal effect in US index ETFs still pay after costs on data that was never looked at?

The test, fixed in advance

  • Buy the close when an ETF finishes in the bottom fifth of its daily range. Sell at the next close. 2 bp of cost on each side.
  • Built on 2000 to 2015. The 2016 to 2026 data was opened once.
  • SPY first, then QQQ, IWM and DIA with identical settings.
  • Pass bar: beat 1,000 random-timing copies at p < 0.05 after costs.
+10.1 bpSPY per trade, 2016 to 2026, after costs
p = 0.041against 1,000 random-timing copies
+13.0 bpthe same rule in training: weaker out of sample
p = 0.24SPY without 2020, a check added after the verdict

QQQ passed clearly (p = 0.001) and held without 2020 (+18.4 bp, p = 0.004). IWM and DIA missed the bar (p = 0.066). ETFs were chosen deliberately: free stock-level data only keeps the companies that survived, which flatters any reversal test.

The callSPY passed the bar written in advance, but the pass rests on one volatile year, so it would not be traded. QQQ is the only version worth a forward test.

Bitcoin · daily · 2013 to 2026

A two-average trend rule against the luckiest of 140 tries

Validated

Is a simple Bitcoin trend rule real, or just the best of many settings that happened to fit the past?

Fixed before the search

  • 140 versions in a grid (fast and slow averages, exit and direction modes), searched on 2018 to 2026.
  • Scoring metric chosen first: Sharpe ratio, so the winner could not be whichever took the most risk.
  • Sealed: Bitcoin 2013 to 2017 from a different exchange, and five other coins, one look each.
  • All 48 possible daily start times tested first. Every one was profitable, so the session was chosen for practicality.

p = 0.0055White's Reality Check: luck alone beat it in fewer than 6 of 1,000 searches
0 of 500random-timing copies beat the rule (median $16.5k vs $338.5k from $10k)
1.31 → 1.00Sharpe, in sample to the out-of-sample average
4 of 5other coins beat buy-and-hold. XRP failed in a sideways market

The callTrend-following itself is the real effect here, and the exact settings are noise, since every one of the 140 versions made money. Size it for the −44% drawdown, not the backtest's −46.7% single path.

Bitcoin · daily · 2018 to 2026

What a stop-loss does to a trend system

Finding recorded

If only the stop moves, how much of a trend system's return does a tight stop give away?

One variable

  • One system: a 20/50-day crossover. The 36 entries were frozen across every version.
  • 500 stop levels from 0.5% to 25%, plus a no-stop control.
  • Limits written into the report: no fees, one market, gaps through a stop not modelled.
+325%with a 2% stop
+648%with no stop at all
22 of 31trades stopped at 2% were back above the stop within 3 days
Full report (PDF) ↗

The callOn a volatile asset, a tight stop mostly cuts trades that would have recovered. Stop distance belongs in the test design, never a default.

Crypto futures · 2021 to 2026

A hand-traded strategy, backtest against live

Live: unproven

Does the live record match the backtest, and does anything beyond the entry explain the winners?

What was measured

  • 100 backtest trades, then 93 live trades on a prop-challenge account, April to August 2026.
  • Entries compared with random entries on the same days, then replicated on two held-out sets.
  • 62 candidate filters tested, with correction for multiple testing. A 19-feature model asked to predict winners.
+0.72Rper trade from the real entries vs −0.10R from random ones (p ≈ 0.0003)
3 of 62filters looked useful: what chance alone produces
0.52–0.56model AUC: winners could not be predicted out of sample

A bug found in my own data

A day-colour tag I had logged on every trade turned out to describe the finished day, not the day at the moment of entry. The at-entry version had no effect. It was look-ahead bias in a field I had written myself.

The callThe entry edge is likely real. No filter earned a place in the rules. It stays unproven until about 100 fresh trades on unseen data.

Gold · 5-minute · May 2025 to Jan 2026

189 trades of a published strategy, after costs

Validation: regime risk

A gold strategy watched 430,000 times on YouTube (by Anish Singh Thakur): what is it worth once costs and luck are taken out?

Method

  • 189 trades taken by hand on chart replay, with my own session window and targets.
  • Costs rebuilt from each venue's published fee schedule; 10,000 reshuffles of the trade order.
  • A mechanical version of the rules coded and run on the same period.
82Rthe gap between venues on identical trades
9 lossesin a row: the median worst streak across 10,000 reshuffles, so normal
t = 1.67for "Mondays made double": one slice of eight, so noise
40%of the gross result came from the 10 best trades
4 of 189hand entries matched by the coded rule, which lost money
+45%gold's own rise over the test window

The callToo strong to be chance, but unproven: one bull regime, and the edge sits in judgement the rules don't yet capture. The next step is a written take-or-skip rule, frozen, then a forward test.

Killed at backtest

Three ideas the data rejected

Killed

Candle patterns · BCH

A candle that predicts the next move

About 5,400 one- and two-feature setups on two years of 1, 5 and 15-minute futures data, with a 70/30 time split. The best setup won 38% of trades on unseen data against 43.7% needed after fees. None was profitable even in training.

In high volatility, moves the other way were just as common (7.7% against 7.8%). The only signal was volatility itself.

Trend pullback · BCH

Rejections from the 200 EMA

4,400 trades in a year with a profit factor of 0.61. Re-fitting the best settings separately for each year from 2020 to 2026 picked long in some years and short in others, with three stop sizes and four targets.

No setting stayed best for two years running, so there was nothing stable to trade.

Value-area levels · BCH

Third of three

Run side by side with two other strategies on the same market for 30 trades each. It made money (+20.7R) and still ranked last.

Research time went to the two stronger candidates instead.

Systems

Taken past the spreadsheet

Automated execution

Four trading bots, run live

Three classic public systems, a Donchian breakout, an 18-period moving average and RSI mean reversion, set to my own rules. The fourth automated my own order-block strategy from the study above. Each was backtested on a sample first, then on new data to rule out look-ahead bias, and then deployed as an always-on service on a rented cloud server.

The bots were stopped when the capital to run them ran out.

Monte Carlo

A career and relocation decision model

1,004 career paths across countries, courses and roles, simulated over 30.9 million lives, with each input given a worst, base and best value and a source.

The first two runs looked too good: about a quarter of simulated finance careers reached a portfolio-manager seat. Both runs were rejected and the model was recalibrated to about one in eleven before any ranking was read. Eight automated checks guard it.

Shake Shack · 2025

The stock loss that wasn't

A stock count came in about £4,000 down. Comparing it period on period showed most of the swing reversed a gain from the previous period, caused by a stock-system outage. Net across both periods the variance was under £40, and one product line carried the real loss. That line became the investigation.

Greggs · 2022 to 2024

A rota built from the sales curve

Research for my MBA dissertation traced lost sales to thin cover at peak and over-production early in the day. A third person at peak, production reset to the hourly sales pattern and ordering to demand followed. Sales rose 20% and waste fell 30% with no extra resources.

Manufacturing · 2018 to 2021

Weekly KPIs for eight products

Performance reports for eight product lines shaped planning across a 33-person plant. Over FY2020, labour cost fell 9%, output rose 4% and revenue grew 11%. Complaints from business customers fell by two thirds.

Working habits

Six rules every study follows

01

Benchmark against random first

An entry rule has to beat random entries on the same days before anything else is asked of it.

02

Seal the test data

Held-out data is opened once. A second look turns it into training data.

03

Count every try

After 140 versions, the bar is the luckiest of 140, and zero stops being the comparison.

04

Costs before conclusions

Fees, spread and funding go in before any result is read. They decide more than the signal often does.

05

Publish the failure

XRP failed the trend rule, and the report shows it on the results page next to the passes.

06

Write for the decision-maker

A result is finished when someone without a quant background can act on it.

First 90 days

Learn the live book before adding to it

Days 1 to 30

Rebuild and reconcile

Learn the platform and the data. Rebuild one live strategy's backtest and match it trade by trade against live fills, so the gap between simulation and execution has a number.

Days 31 to 60

Attribute

Trade-level attribution across the live strategies: where returns come from, and where costs, liquidity or execution take them back. Each fix ranked by its expected impact.

Days 61 to 90

One new strategy, end to end

One hypothesis through the full path, with the test written down before the data is opened. The result goes to the Managing Director with its impact, its risks and a recommendation.

Questions you might have

Why Quant Lab?

The role owns a strategy from the first hypothesis to live trading, with no layer between the analyst and the decision. That is the loop these studies have run on a smaller scale, without the capital or the execution behind them.

Quant Lab's own filter, where only the strongest risk-adjusted strategies reach live capital, matches how my studies end. Most of my findings have overturned an earlier view of mine, which is the kind of work the role describes.

What will I bring with me?
  • Test design: random benchmarks, sealed data and corrections for searching, set up before results are seen.
  • Cost realism: venue-by-venue fee and spread models, applied before a result is read.
  • Trade-level analysis: streak and drawdown distributions by reshuffling, concentration in a few trades, slices tested for significance.
  • Live discipline: bots run as always-on services, and a hand-traded strategy tracked against its own backtest.
  • Operating rhythm: eight years of weekly numbers reported to someone who acted on them.
  • Plain writing: reports that explain every term for a reader outside the field.
